TRANSMISSION 009

The Courage Loop
Rewriting Fear at Runtime

A person writes a few sentences. The Emotions Cell matches words against fixed lists, offers a reframe from a nine-row table, asks up to three questions and suggests rituals. This note says what that pipeline is in code, what two January session records actually show, what was added in July, and which claims in the earlier drafts the record does not support.

First published May 2026·Revised November 2026·Field note·Emotional systems·11 min read

What this note describes, and when it was written. It describes the Emotions Cell as it stands in the Brain's code on 10 November 2026, the two session records the cell wrote on 20 and 24 January 2026, and the measuring and reflection work recorded on 3 July 2026. It was written on 10 November 2026.

Revised 10 November 2026. Rewritten to the transmission standard. Transmissions 07 and 08 were earlier drafts of the same idea; they stay online as short pages that point here. Every figure comes from the cell's source, its tests, or a dated record. The detector below is a port written for this page and checked against the source by reading; it is not the cell itself. The page makes no network calls.

Correction, 10 November 2026

The earlier text of this note, and of Transmissions 07 and 08, said or implied the following. None of it is supported by the record, and each point is withdrawn.

  • That "50% intensity" was a measured strength of feeling. It is what a formula returns when one keyword appears once (section 02).
  • That the loop "runs continuously within the autonomous organism" and processes the organism's own emotions. The cell takes a person's journal text when asked. No record shows a machine with emotions, and the 8 November audit classed the cell as code that has not run on the machine the organism now lives on.
  • That detection was natural language processing, rituals were personalised, and beliefs were rewritten by "cognitive refactoring algorithms". They are substring matches, templates by theme, and a lookup table.
  • Stress inoculation, physiological sensors, "an autonomous system that not only thinks but feels", and "the next frontier of artificial consciousness". No record supports these; they are removed, not softened.

The title is kept. "Courage" is the name of one ritual in the cell, and "loop" is the name of the mantra it prints. The first-published date is 16 May 2026, the day the pages first appear in the site's history; the earlier label "April 2026" is replaced.

01

A coaching pipeline with a loud name

What the loop is, and who runs it

The Courage Loop is the name for how the Brain's Emotions Cell treats a piece of journal text. Someone writes a few sentences. The cell looks for emotional themes, looks for a short list of limiting-belief phrases, offers a reframe for each phrase it finds, asks coaching questions, and lists rituals to try. Then it writes what it did into a session record. Every verb in that paragraph is code that exists. None of them is a machine feeling anything.

Who is the courage for? A person. The cell's charter is plain about it: it is the part of the system that meets a person on a hard day, and it never claims therapeutic authority. The earlier drafts of this note put the fear inside the organism. The record puts it where it always was, in the words of the human typing. The machine's side is pattern matching and a library of templates.

Why keep the metaphor at all? Because the design borrows engineering language on purpose. "Every day is a loop. Rewrite the code." is the cell's mantra, and it is printed at the end of every session record. A name makes an idea easy to carry. This note uses "courage", "fear" and "rewrite" as language for a design, and next to each one it says what exists.

Below is a port of the cell's detection step, written for this page. It uses the same word lists and the same formulas, runs in your browser, and sends nothing anywhere. Type a few sentences, or press a sample, and see exactly what the cell would match. It is a demonstration of a word list, not a coach and not a diagnosis.

Detector port · same lists, same formulas · stays in your browser

Try the third sample: "made" contains "mad", so a calm sentence about dinner is read as anger. Try typing an apostrophe from a phone keyboard in "I can't": the curly form does not match the plain one in the list.

Three things to notice. Each theme reports at most one pattern, and only for the first word on its list that appears. A word counts wherever it appears, inside other words included. And the cell has no idea what a sentence means: "I'm not afraid" and "I'm afraid" match the same fear word.

02

Detection is a word list

Six themes, one formula

The cell checks for six themes: fear, anger, joy, sadness, growth and limiting belief. Each has a short list of trigger words, read straight from the code (press a theme below). A theme is "detected" when any one of its words appears in lower-cased text.

Intensity is one line of arithmetic: 0.3 plus 0.2 for each time the matched word appears, capped at 1.0. One appearance gives 50 percent. Two give 70, three give 90, four or more give 100. That is the whole of "intensity" in the January session records, and it measures how often a word was repeated, not how strongly anyone felt.

This corrects the earlier reading of the 24 January record. It said fear ("anxious") and joy ("excited") registered "at 50% intensity: the optimal state, adaptive resonance". Both numbers are the same because each word appeared once. A tie created by a formula is not a balance of feelings, and "adaptive resonance" has no definition in the code. Likewise "No limiting beliefs identified" in that record means only that none of nine fixed phrases appeared in the text. It is not an audit of anyone's beliefs.

What a theme weight is

Since July the cell also holds a weight for each theme on a positive-to-negative axis: joy +1.0, growth +0.6, limiting belief -0.7, anger -0.8, sadness -0.9, fear -1.0. They are chosen constants used to combine theme densities into one number. They are not discovered properties of emotion.

03

Nine phrases, nine reframes

A lookup table, not a rewrite

The earlier drafts called this step "cognitive refactoring at the source-code level". In code it is a table. The cell holds nine limiting-belief phrases. When one appears in the entry, the cell returns the reframe and the affirmation written for that phrase, and it never composes a new one. Every mapping carries a confidence of 0.75 because the number is a constant set in the code, not an estimate. Open a card to read the pair.

The reframes are an author's stance, in the voice of the project's mantra: "I can't" becomes "I am learning how to", "I'm afraid" becomes "Fear is information, not a stop sign. I move forward with it." That is a coaching choice, and a debatable one for some phrases. The cell's charter forbids clinical claims, and this note makes none: no record measures whether any reframe helps anyone.

Fear sits in two lists. The word "afraid" belongs to the fear theme, and the phrase "I'm afraid" is also one of the nine belief phrases. An entry containing it gets both a fear pattern and a belief mapping. That is how the 20 January record came to hold a fear pattern and two mapped beliefs from a single entry.

04

Rituals, and what the January records show

Two session records, read against the code

Rituals are templates chosen by theme. A fear pattern yields Courage Activation: write down one fear and one small action to face it, take that action, ten minutes, daily. A limiting-belief pattern yields Belief Rewrite: read the reframe aloud three times and visualise it, five minutes, twice daily. Sadness yields a gratitude exercise, and growth yields a journaling prompt. Joy and anger yield none. Every session adds The Loop Reset: repeat the mantra, then choose one thing to rewrite today, three minutes, daily.

Earlier drafts called these "deployment procedures" and "a CI/CD pipeline for consciousness". They are text. Nothing in the cell checks whether a ritual was done, schedules a reminder, or follows up. "Rewrite the code" means whatever the person decides it means: a habit, a conversation, a line of software. The person is the only runtime here.

    05
    July: measuring, remembering, reflecting back
    
        
    Recorded 3 July 2026 · closed in tests, not in service
    
        
    
          
    On 3 July 2026 the cell gained four things, described in that day's engineering note. First, a measurement: a journal entry is scored into a state with a valence (negative to positive), an arousal (calm to activated) and a density per theme. The scoring is deterministic and uses no network: the same text gives the same reading. Second, each reading is stored in the Brain's typed memory with a user tag. Third, a trend: the first half of a user's readings is compared with the second half, and the direction is "rising" or "falling" when the mean valence moves by more than 0.05, otherwise "flat". Fourth, a reflection: one coaching question, chosen from a template by trend direction and dominant theme.
    
          
    The reflection can ask an optional language-model engine to write the question instead. If the engine is absent or fails, the template is used, so the loop works with no provider at all. The loop's shape is: a reading, a prompt, the person's next entry, a new reading. The cell does not decide anything about the person. It asks a question.
    
          
    The proof is eighteen tests, eleven on measurement and seven on reflection, recorded as passing in that day's note. One of them closes the loop on synthetic entries: a fearful reading produces a prompt, a recovery entry follows, and the trend turns to rising. It shows the mechanism works on invented text. It is not evidence that the mechanism helps a real person. Use the panel to read the four templates the cell chooses between.

    06
    What this note does not claim
    
        
    The boundary, stated plainly
    
        
    
          
      
            
    • No machine feels. Nothing here fears, hopes, copes, or is conscious. The cell scores words in a person's text.
      • 
            
    • No therapy and no diagnosis. The cell is a journaling aid with fixed text. No outcome was measured, so no claim is made that any ritual or reframe works.
      • 
            
    • No continuous operation. The cell runs when it is called with an entry. Earlier wording about constant scanning is withdrawn.
      • 
            
    • No understanding. Matching is by substring against fixed lists. Negation, sarcasm, other languages and a curly apostrophe all defeat it.
      • 
            
    • No live service shown. Tests pass on synthetic text; the audit marks the cell a placeholder on the current machine.
      • 
            
    • No roadmap. Predictive emotional modelling, ritual pre-generation and physiological sensors appeared in the earlier draft as a future. No record plans them, and they are removed.
      • 
          
    
          
    What stands: a small deterministic pipeline, two honest session records, eighteen passing tests recorded in July, and a name that is a metaphor for all of it.
