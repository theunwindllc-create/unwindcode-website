01

Done means a receipt, not a claim

The planning document's rule, written 8 November 2026

The planning document's own definition states the rule plainly: done means a tested, end-to-end result with a receipt, never a document or profile created. That standard already governs three parts of the organism: a rule for what counts as an unbroken proof chain, a shared job board where a job is not finished until it names its own result, and an audit that checked a claimed roster of working agents against which ones actually had a running trace.

02

A gap is measured, not assumed away

What "unbroken" actually has to mean

The chain-tolerance rule treats "unbroken" as a property of the design, not of conditions outside its control. A gap in proof is allowed, within a bound, as long as it is automatically detected, explained in a dated record with its cause, and closed by the next successful proof carrying forward from where it left off — rather than pretending gaps don't happen, or treating any single gap as fatal.

03

What the receipts actually show

Baselines, 8 November 2026

By the planning document's own count on 8 November 2026: zero executed tasks carried a verified receipt that week; one hand of several active on the machine had a receipt in the prior 30 days against zero on a roster of planned agent seats; zero of fourteen prior nightly checks came back clean; roughly seventy open approval items waited, the oldest over two months old; verified revenue stood at zero.

      Claimed progress exists on paper. Progress exists only where a receipt is.

      04

      What this costs in speed

      The price of a receipts-only count

      A system that counts only receipts will, by construction, show low or zero progress on anything not yet receipted — even if real, unverified work exists behind that number. The rule accepts that cost on purpose rather than crediting an unverified claim. Most measured categories read at or near zero right now, because the rule refuses to credit anything else.

      05

      Why the rule stays, anyway

      What an audit elsewhere on the record already proved

      Counting claims instead of receipts is exactly the failure an audit elsewhere on the record already caught once, when a far smaller number of agents turned out to have an actual running trace than had been claimed. A receipts-only count cannot repeat that failure, because it never credits a claim it cannot trace.
